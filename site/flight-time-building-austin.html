Fly Independently or Build Time Together

The plan is for eligible instrument and commercial students to fly independently, or to request pairing with another student working toward similar training goals at a similar stage. Either way, flights would be planned around your own logbook and what you're actually trying to accomplish — not a generic hour-building package.

Important distinction

Pairing is optional, subject to availability and compatibility — it is not guaranteed. Flying with another pilot does not by itself make a flight eligible for shared logging credit, and it does not mean both pilots can log identical total time, PIC time, or cross-country time for the same flight. What each pilot can actually log depends on their specific role in that flight under the applicable FAA regulations — not simply on being aboard together. Aircraft checkout, insurance coverage, and operating eligibility for any pairing arrangement also remain subject to final policy and are not yet confirmed.

For Instrument Rating Students

Purposeful cross-country flying builds the kind of real-world judgment instrument training depends on — working actual weather decisions, alternates, fuel planning, and routing instead of flying hours just to log them. That makes you a sharper, more prepared instrument student, even before you touch IFR-specific training.

Important distinction

General time-building flights are not the same as logged instrument time. Under 14 CFR 61.65, instrument time only counts when it's flown in actual instrument conditions, under a view-limiting device with a qualified safety pilot, or in an approved aviation training device. A VFR cross-country, however valuable for planning practice, does not by itself add to the 40 hours of instrument time the rating requires.

For the full instrument rating experience requirements — instrument time, cross-country, and dual instruction minimums — see Instrument Rating Training.

For Commercial Pilot Students

Commercial applicants need real flight experience behind them, not just a training syllabus — total flight time and cross-country pilot-in-command time accumulate through flying, and purposeful time building is one practical way to work toward those minimums while you're between other training milestones.

Important distinction

General time building adds to your total flight time and cross-country PIC time, and that's genuinely useful progress toward the 14 CFR 61.129 minimums. But it does not automatically satisfy every requirement — the complex, turbine, or technically advanced aircraft training §61.129(a)(3)(ii) requires, for example, has to come from specific structured training in that category of aircraft, not from general hour-building.

For the full commercial pilot experience requirements, see Commercial Pilot Training.
