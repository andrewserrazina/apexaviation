How to Actually Use
the ACS to Study.

Most applicants have opened it. Few have used it as a study tool rather than a reference document they skim once.

What the ACS Replaced, and Why It's Different

The Airman Certification Standards (ACS) replaced the older Practical Test Standards (PTS) for private pilot certification. The PTS mostly listed what skills would be tested. The ACS goes further: it explicitly integrates three dimensions for every task — Knowledge (what you need to understand), Risk Management (the hazards associated with that knowledge and how to mitigate them), and Skill (what you need to be able to do). That integration is exactly why the oral exam asks the questions it does — a DPE evaluating a task isn't just checking a fact, they're checking whether you understand the risk behind it too.

The full document is organized into Areas of Operation — broad categories like Preflight Preparation, Airport Operations, Takeoffs and Landings, Navigation, and Emergency Operations — each broken into specific Tasks. Every Task lists its own Knowledge, Risk Management, and Skill elements, each with a reference code you'll see used throughout FAA guidance material (for example, codes referencing Preflight Preparation's Pilot Qualifications task).

Reading It as a Study Tool, Not Just a Reference

Most students treat the ACS as something to open once, skim, and then set aside in favor of separate study materials. That undersells it. The ACS is the actual document your DPE is required to test against — every oral exam question and every flight maneuver traces back to a specific line in it. Using it as your primary study outline, rather than a background reference, means you're never studying something the checkride won't touch, and you're never missing something it will.

A practical approach: go through Preflight Preparation's tasks one at a time — Pilot Qualifications, Airworthiness Requirements, Weather Information, Cross-Country Flight Planning, National Airspace System, Performance and Limitations, Operation of Systems, and Human Factors — and for each one, write out (or say out loud) an answer to every Knowledge and Risk Management element listed. If you can't answer one cleanly, that's your study list — not a vague sense that "weather" needs work, but the specific element inside weather that's actually weak.

Ground Knowledge vs. Flight Maneuvers

Not every Area of Operation gets tested the same way. Preflight Preparation is almost entirely oral-exam material — eligibility, documents, weather, planning, airspace, performance, systems, and aeromedical factors all get discussed and explained, not physically demonstrated. Areas like Takeoffs and Landings, Performance Maneuvers, Slow Flight and Stalls, and Basic Instrument Maneuvers are primarily flight-portion tasks — demonstrated in the airplane with your instructor and evaluated during the flight, not asked about at length during the oral (though the DPE may still ask a knowledge or risk-management question tied to a maneuver before you fly it).

Navigation and Emergency Operations tend to bridge both — you'll discuss navigation planning and emergency procedures knowledge during the oral, and then demonstrate related skills (diversions, simulated engine failures) during the flight. Knowing which of your ACS areas are oral-heavy versus flight-heavy helps you allocate study time correctly instead of over-preparing orally for something that's mostly a stick-and-rudder skill.

A Practical ACS Study Checklist

Before moving on from any Area of Operation, you should be able to: state each Task's Knowledge elements without notes; explain the specific real-world risk each Risk Management element is addressing (not just name it); and, for tasks with a Skill element, describe what correct performance actually looks like. If any of those three checks fails for a given task, it isn't done yet — regardless of how many times you've read through it.
