Sample Mock Oral
Exam Questions.

A real DPE won't ask questions from a script, but these are the kinds of questions and follow-ups that come up constantly across every knowledge area.

How to Use This List

These are original sample questions written to reflect the style and depth of a typical private pilot oral exam — not a leaked or copied question bank. Every one of them maps to a real ACS knowledge or risk-management element, and every one has a natural follow-up that a good examiner will ask if your first answer is too shallow. Read each question, answer it out loud before reading the guidance underneath, and if you can't get through the follow-up cleanly, that's a real gap — not a maybe.

Pilot Qualifications & Documents

Q: What documents are you required to have with you for this flight, and how do you know they're current? A strong answer names the certificate and medical (or reliance on BasicMed, if applicable), photo ID, and — for the aircraft — airworthiness certificate, registration, radio station license if operating internationally, and the operating limitations. The follow-up that trips people up: "How do you personally verify currency, not just possession?" That means being able to state your own flight review date, medical expiration, and how you'd check the aircraft's inspection status (annual, 100-hour if applicable, transponder/pitot-static/ELT checks) from the logbooks, not just from memory.

Q: You haven't flown in four months. Are you legal to carry a passenger today? This is really a currency question wearing a documents costume. The answer requires walking through the three-takeoffs-and-landings-in-90-days rule, distinguishing day vs. night currency if the flight will occur after evening civil twilight, and being explicit that "legal" and "safe" aren't the same thing — a good answer addresses both.

Weather

Q: Walk me through how you'd determine whether today's weather supports this flight, start to finish. This question rewards a process, not a fact dump. A strong answer starts with the big picture (synopsis, area forecast discussion, any AIRMETs/SIGMETs along the route) before narrowing to specific METARs and TAFs at departure, destination, and an alternate. The common failure mode is reciting individual weather products in isolation without ever tying them back to "so is this flight actually a good idea."

Q: The AWOS reports a ceiling of 3,500 broken and visibility of 4 miles. Are you legal to fly VFR in this class of airspace? This tests memorized weather minimums applied to a specific number, not the ability to recite the whole table from memory. Whatever the answer, the follow-up is always "and if that number were 500 feet lower, what changes?" — testing whether you understand where the line actually is, not just that a line exists.

Airspace & Cross-Country Planning

Q: Your planned route crosses from Class E into Class C airspace near a towered airport. What do you need to do differently, and when? A complete answer covers the equipment and communication requirements for Class C (two-way radio contact established before entry, appropriate transponder/ADS-B), not just "call the tower." The examiner is listening for whether you know the difference between "requesting" and "establishing" contact — you need to hear your call sign back before entering, not just make the call.

Q: How did you choose your cruising altitude for this cross-country, and what would change your mind? This tests hemispheric cruising altitude rules (odd/even thousands plus 500 for VFR based on magnetic course), but a strong answer also brings in terrain, winds aloft, oxygen requirements above 12,500 feet, and airspace shelves along the route — not just the rule in isolation.

Aircraft Systems & Performance

Q: Explain how the fuel system in your aircraft works, from the tanks to the engine. Examiners ask this because it's a real gap for a lot of applicants who can fly the airplane but can't describe it. A strong answer traces the actual physical path — tank selector or gravity feed, fuel pump(s), primer if equipped, mixture control — specific to the training aircraft, not a generic description of "how fuel systems work" in general.

Q: Today's density altitude is significantly higher than the field elevation. What does that actually do to your takeoff performance, and how do you find out by how much? The answer needs to name the specific effects (longer ground roll, reduced climb rate, reduced prop and engine efficiency) and then demonstrate pulling real numbers from the POH performance charts for today's conditions — not just saying "performance decreases."

Aeromedical & Human Factors

Q: What's the difference between hypoxia and hyperventilation, and how would you tell which one you were experiencing in flight? Both can produce similar symptoms, which is exactly the point of the question — a strong answer distinguishes them by cause (insufficient oxygen vs. excess CO2 elimination from rapid breathing) and by the practical test (supplemental oxygen and descent help hypoxia; controlled breathing helps hyperventilation), plus recognizes that you may not reliably self-diagnose which one you have while it's happening.

Q: Describe the IMSAFE checklist and how you'd apply it to yourself right now. The trap here is reciting the acronym without actually applying it. A strong answer runs through Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, and Emotion as they genuinely apply to the applicant that day — a real self-assessment, not a recitation.

Emergency Operations

Q: Your engine quits at 2,500 feet AGL over unfamiliar terrain. Walk me through exactly what you do, in order. This question is really testing prioritization under pressure: establish best glide speed first, then pick a landing site, then attempt a restart if time and altitude allow, then communicate (transponder 7700, radio call) — and only once those are handled, run the appropriate checklist. Applicants who list the checklist items first, before securing an airspeed and a landing spot, are demonstrating the wrong priority order even if every individual item is technically correct.
