The 30 Days Before
Your Checkride.

Not more studying — different studying. The final month has its own priorities.

Day 30–22: Find Your Real Weak Areas

If your checkride is 30 days out, you've presumably already done most of your ground school and initial ACS study. This window isn't for learning new material from scratch — it's for a systematic pass through every ACS Area of Operation to find out, honestly, which ones are actually weak. Don't rely on a gut feeling here; work through real practice questions across every category and pay attention to where you hesitate or give an incomplete answer, not just where you're flat-out wrong.

By day 22, you should have a specific, written list of your weak areas — not "weather" broadly, but the specific piece (METAR decoding, icing conditions, a specific airspace rule) that's actually shaky. That list is what the next phase is built around.

Day 21–14: Targeted Repair, Not General Review

Spend this window almost entirely on the weak-area list from the previous phase. This is the highest-leverage study time you have, because it's the only phase specifically aimed at closing real gaps rather than reviewing material you already know. Resist the temptation to re-study your strong areas here just because it feels comfortable and productive — it's a worse use of these two weeks than deliberately grinding on what's actually shaky.

Start layering in scenario-based practice during this window too: work an actual cross-country flight plan for a route similar to what your DPE might assign, run a real weight and balance calculation for your checkride aircraft at a realistic loading, and talk through an emergency scenario out loud rather than just reciting the checklist from memory.

Day 13–7: Full Mock Orals

This is the week for at least one complete mock oral exam under realistic conditions — two to three hours, no notes, a DPE-style examiner who asks real follow-up questions and occasionally changes a scenario's variables mid-question. If you can, run a second one a few days later focused specifically on whatever the first mock oral exposed as still weak.

This is also the week to finalize your logbook and documents: confirm every required endorsement is present and correctly dated, confirm your medical certificate is current through your checkride date, and gather your ARROW documents (Airworthiness certificate, Registration, Radio license if required, Operating limitations, Weight and balance) so nothing is a last-minute scramble.

Day 6–2: Light Review Only

Ease off new material almost entirely. Do brief, targeted review of your weak-area list — enough to keep it fresh, not enough to induce new anxiety about material you'd otherwise be confident in. This is also a reasonable window to finalize your specific cross-country flight plan for the checkride, since your DPE will typically assign the route a few days out.

Get normal sleep this week. A rushed, anxious final stretch tends to produce worse recall on checkride day than a calm one, even with slightly less raw study time.

The Day Before and Day Of

Do a final, brief pass over your weak-area notes — not new studying, just a refresher. Confirm the aircraft's airworthiness status, confirm weather for the actual flight, and have your documents organized and ready to hand over. Treat checkride day as a normal flying day: eat normally, arrive with time to spare, and remember that the oral exam is a conversation about material you've now spent thirty days actually preparing for — not a surprise.
