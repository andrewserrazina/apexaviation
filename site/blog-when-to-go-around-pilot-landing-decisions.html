The Go-Around:
Why Great Pilots Know When to Try Again

Good landings begin with good decisions — and sometimes the best decision is not to land at all.

There's a moment on final approach when every pilot has a choice.

The runway is ahead. The airplane is descending. The landing checklist is complete. Everything seems to be coming together.

But something isn't quite right.

Maybe the airplane is a little too fast. Maybe the approach is unstable. Maybe a gust of wind has pushed you off centerline. Or perhaps another aircraft hasn't cleared the runway.

Whatever the reason, the picture no longer looks the way it should.

And that's when good pilots make one of the most important decisions in aviation.

They go around.

A Go-Around Is Not a Failed Landing

Somewhere along the way, many student pilots develop the idea that a go-around means they did something wrong.

They were supposed to land, and they didn't.

But that's not how good aeronautical decision-making works.

A go-around is a normal maneuver, a fundamental skill, and an important safety tool. It allows a pilot to discontinue an approach or landing when the conditions are no longer acceptable.

Think about it this way: the goal of an approach isn't simply to get the wheels onto the runway. The goal is to complete a safe landing.

If the conditions for a safe landing aren't there, continuing the approach doesn't accomplish that goal.

Going around does.

The Stabilized Approach: Your First Line of Defense

One of the best ways to make better landing decisions is to establish clear expectations before reaching the runway.

A stabilized approach generally means the airplane is:

  • On the intended flightpath and aligned appropriately with the runway.
  • At an appropriate airspeed and descent rate.
  • In the correct configuration for landing.
  • Requiring only normal, manageable corrections.
  • Positioned to land within the intended touchdown area.

The specific criteria and decision points should reflect the aircraft, operating procedures, and conditions.

When those criteria aren't met, a go-around may be the safest choice.

The important part is deciding before the situation becomes urgent.

A pilot who recognizes an unstable approach early has more time, more altitude, and more options.

Why Pilots Sometimes Hesitate

If go-arounds are so useful, why do pilots occasionally resist them?

Part of the answer is a psychological tendency called plan continuation bias.

Once we've committed to a plan, we naturally want to finish it.

You've entered the traffic pattern. You've configured the airplane. You've flown the approach. The runway is right there.

It can feel easier to make one more correction than to abandon the landing attempt and start again.

But aviation rewards sound decisions, not stubbornness.

A pilot who continues an unstable approach because they're determined to land is accepting a risk that may be completely unnecessary.

A pilot who recognizes the problem and goes around is demonstrating judgment.

That's an important distinction.

The Go-Around Requires Practice

Knowing when to go around is only half the skill.

You also need to know how to execute the maneuver correctly.

A go-around involves transitioning the airplane from an approach or landing configuration back into a climb. Depending on the aircraft, that means managing power, pitch, airspeed, configuration, coordination, and the intended flightpath.

The exact sequence matters.

Adding power changes the forces acting on the airplane. Pitch and trim requirements may change. Configuration changes must be managed carefully, and the pilot needs to maintain directional control while establishing a safe climb.

That's why the go-around should never be treated as an afterthought.

Practice it with your instructor. Understand your aircraft's published procedures. Learn to recognize the cues that indicate a go-around is necessary.

Most importantly, practice making the decision without hesitation.

A maneuver that's familiar in training is much easier to execute confidently when conditions demand it.

The Runway Will Still Be There

Imagine you're approaching the runway on a gusty afternoon.

The airplane is moving around more than expected. Your airspeed is fluctuating. You're working harder than usual to maintain the desired approach path.

You might still be able to land.

But being able to land and having a good reason to continue are two different things.

A go-around gives you the opportunity to reset.

You can reestablish a safe flightpath, reassess the conditions, and decide whether another approach is appropriate.

Sometimes the next approach is better.

Sometimes the conditions suggest waiting, choosing another runway, or diverting.

The point is that you haven't allowed the desire to finish one landing attempt to eliminate your other options.

Confidence Is Knowing When to Say No

One of the most valuable lessons in flight training is that confidence doesn't mean forcing an outcome.

It means understanding your aircraft, recognizing changing conditions, and making decisions based on what the situation requires.

A confident pilot doesn't need to prove they can salvage every approach.

They know when to continue.

They know when to make a correction.

And they know when to go around.

The same mindset applies far beyond landing. Weather decisions, fuel planning, aircraft discrepancies, and changing flight conditions all require the willingness to reconsider a plan.

Sometimes the most professional thing a pilot can say is, "We're going to try that again."

Make the Go-Around a Normal Decision

The next time you're practicing landings, think about your go-around criteria before turning final.

What does a stable approach look like in your aircraft?

What conditions would make you discontinue the landing?

What procedure will you follow?

Having those answers in advance reduces uncertainty when you need to act.

And remember: nobody gets extra points for forcing an airplane onto the runway.

A good pilot knows how to land. A great pilot also knows when not to.

Fly smart. Stay proficient. And never be afraid to go around.
