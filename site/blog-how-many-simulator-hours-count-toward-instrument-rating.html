How Many Simulator Hours
Count Toward Your Rating?

Under Part 61, you can credit up to 20 hours of AATD time toward your instrument rating. Here's exactly how it works.

One of the most common questions instrument students ask is simple: how much of my training can happen in the simulator?

The short answer is more than most pilots realize.

The FAA Rule: Part 61.65(i)

Under 14 CFR §61.65(i), a pilot training toward an instrument rating under Part 61 may log up to 20 hours of flight training time in an Aviation Training Device (ATD) — provided that device meets the FAA's AATD (Advanced Aviation Training Device) qualification standard.

That's out of the 40 total instrument flight hours required (15 of which must be with an authorized instructor). So in a well-structured program, half of your instrument flight training can legally happen in the sim.

What Qualifies as an AATD?

Not every sim counts. A basic home flight simulator doesn't qualify. An AATD is a specific category of FAA-approved device that meets defined fidelity standards for avionics, flight model, and system simulation — devices like the Redbird FMX are common examples at flight schools around the country. Every hour flown in a genuine AATD counts toward the 20-hour allowance, provided it's logged with an authorized instructor and covers the appropriate training tasks.

Why the Allowance Exists

The financial math is straightforward for schools that use it well. Aircraft time runs significantly more per hour than simulator time. Substituting 20 hours of aircraft time with 20 hours of qualified sim time saves real money without sacrificing — and arguably improving — the quality of training, because a simulator lets a student do things that aren't safe or practical in an actual aircraft:

  • Pause and reset at the moment of a mistake
  • Repeat an approach until it's automatic
  • Fail systems without risk — vacuum pump, gyros, pitot heat
  • Practice in actual IMC without needing actual IMC

Students who use AATD time well typically arrive at the aircraft phase with stronger scan habits, better situational awareness, and more confident radio technique.

Apex's Own Approach: Real Aircraft, No Simulator

If you train with Apex, every instrument hour is flown for real in our dedicated training aircraft — the expected aircraft is a Piper PA-28, currently under lease negotiation — and we don't operate a simulator or credit AATD time toward our own students' instrument training. That's a deliberate choice, not a gap: we think hours flown for real build a kind of judgment a simulator can't fully substitute, and keeping the training honest to a single aircraft with a single instructor keeps the curriculum focused.

That said, the 20-hour AATD allowance is real, FAA-sanctioned value for pilots training elsewhere who do have simulator access. If your school offers a qualified AATD, understanding exactly how the credit works — and making sure your instructor is logging it correctly — is worth doing regardless of where you train.

The Bottom Line

If you're pursuing your instrument rating through a program with AATD access and you're not using the full legal allowance, you're leaving a real cost-and-efficiency advantage on the table. If you're training with Apex, your instrument hours are flown entirely in the aircraft — and the curriculum is built around that from day one.
