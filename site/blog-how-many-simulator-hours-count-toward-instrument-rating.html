How Many Simulator Hours
Count Toward Your Rating?

Part 61 allows different instrument-rating credit limits for eligible basic and advanced aviation training devices. Learn what counts.

One of the most common questions instrument students ask is simple: how much of my training can happen in the simulator?

The short answer is more than most pilots realize.

The FAA Rule: Part 61.65(i)

Under 14 CFR §61.65(i), a typical Part 61 instrument applicant may credit up to 10 hours in an authorized BATD or 20 hours in an authorized AATD, subject to the FAA authorization and the regulation's instructor, logging, and other conditions. The credit rules also distinguish aviation training devices from flight training devices and full flight simulators.

That's out of the 40 total instrument flight hours required (15 of which must be with an authorized instructor). So in a well-structured program, half of your instrument flight training can legally happen in the sim.

What Qualifies as an AATD?

Not every simulator counts: a home flight simulator is not automatically an approved aviation training device. Confirm the specific BATD/AATD's current FAA authorization, eligible training tasks, instructor participation, and proper logbook entries. The device model name alone is not sufficient.

Why the Allowance Exists

Eligible simulator sessions can reduce some training costs and enable repeatable scenarios that would be unsuitable in an aircraft. Device-credit limits do not guarantee a one-for-one aircraft-hours replacement or identical proficiency; actual training and savings vary.

  • Pause and reset at the moment of a mistake
  • Repeat an approach until it's automatic
  • Fail systems without risk — vacuum pump, gyros, pitot heat
  • Practice in actual IMC without needing actual IMC

Students who use AATD time well typically arrive at the aircraft phase with stronger scan habits, better situational awareness, and more confident radio technique.

That said, the 20-hour AATD allowance is real, FAA-sanctioned value for pilots training elsewhere who do have simulator access. If your school offers a qualified AATD, understanding exactly how the credit works — and making sure your instructor is logging it correctly — is worth doing regardless of where you train.

The Bottom Line

If you're pursuing an instrument rating, ask your CFII how approved device experience applies to your program and how much aircraft practice you need to become proficient. The best training plan is not simply the one that maximizes device-credit hours.
